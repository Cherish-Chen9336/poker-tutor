## Lesson 1: The goal and the flow of a hand

**The goal is to win chips.** Each hand, players put chips into a shared pile in the middle called the **pot**. Whoever wins the hand takes the pot. The chips in front of you are your **stack**.

**There are two ways to win a pot:**
1. **Everyone else folds.** To **fold** means to give up the hand. If every other player folds, you win the pot at once, without showing your cards. This happens in many hands.
2. **The showdown.** If two or more players are still in after the last betting round, they show their cards. The best five-card hand wins; equal hands share the pot.

**A hand has four betting rounds.** Before each one, cards are dealt:

[Visual: the existing timeline (Preflop: 2 private cards each; Flop: 3 shared cards; Turn: a 4th; River: the 5th; "then a betting round" after each), plus a side note on it: "Everyone else folds → the hand ends at once, with no more cards."]

In each betting round, players take turns to bet, match a bet, or fold (Lesson 5 explains each move). A betting round ends when everyone still in has matched the biggest bet or is all-in, and everyone who can still act has had a turn. Then the hand moves on.

**Before any cards are dealt,** two players must put in forced bets called **blinds**, so there's always something to win. Lesson 4 explains who pays them.

## Lesson 2: Your cards and the board

You get **2 private cards** (your **hole cards**). Nobody else sees them. The **5 shared cards** in the middle are called the **board**, and everyone can use them.

**Your hand is the best 5 cards out of these 7.** You can use both hole cards, just one, or even none:

[Visual: the existing three rows, each showing your 2 cards and the board with the 5 cards that count highlighted. New result labels:
- **Uses 2 of your cards:** three fives (5♣ 5♦ 5♥, plus K and 9).
- **Uses 1:** a flush in hearts (A♥ plus the board's four hearts). Your 3♣ doesn't count.
- **Uses 0:** A-A-K-K-Q, all from the board. Your jacks don't play, so an opponent holding 3♣ 2♦ has the same hand and you split the pot.]

**Read the board for what's possible.** Ask: "What's the best hand anyone could have here?" The best possible hand on a board is called **the nuts**. On K♠ 9♥ 4♣ 2♦ 7♠, nobody can have a straight or a flush, and with no pair on the board, nobody can have a full house. The nuts is three kings, from a player holding two kings.

**Starting hands.** There are 1,326 possible two-card combinations. You'll hear names like "pocket pair" (two cards of the same rank, such as 7♣ 7♥), "suited" (both cards in the same suit), and "AK" (ace-king). A pocket pair comes about once every 17 hands.

## Lesson 3: Hand rankings

From strongest to weakest, with how often each comes up:

[Visual: the existing 10-row ladder, unchanged.]

**How often** means: how often 7 random cards (your 2 plus the 5 on the board) make that hand as their best, counted exactly over all 133,784,560 possible 7-card combinations. It's not your chance of *winning*: your opponents get 7 cards too.

**Notice:** with 7 cards, one pair (43.8%) and two pair (23.5%) are more common than no pair at all (17.4%). That's why a single pair is often not enough to win. The ranking comes from 5-card poker; with 7 cards only "no pair" moves out of that order.

**Three rules that surprise beginners:**
1. **The ace can be low in a straight:** A-2-3-4-5 is the lowest straight. A straight can't wrap around: Q-K-A-2-3 is not a straight.
2. **Suits never break ties.** On Q♦ J♣ 10♥ 2♠ 3♣, A♥ K♥ and A♠ K♠ make the same straight and tie. (A suit only matters when five cards of it make a flush. On a board with three hearts, A♥ K♥ would make a flush and win.)
3. **A flush beats a straight.** Flushes are harder to make.

When two hands are the same kind, Lesson 7 shows how they're compared.

## Lesson 4: Seats, the button, and the blinds

**The button** marks the "dealer" for this hand. It moves one seat to the left (clockwise) after every hand, so everyone takes turns.

**The blinds** are the forced bets that start each pot, paid before anyone sees cards:
- The **small blind (SB)**, just left of the button, puts in 1 chip.
- The **big blind (BB)**, next to the small blind, puts in 2 chips.

Posting a blind happens automatically before the cards are dealt; it doesn't replace that player's turn.

**You'll play heads-up (two players) first.** There, the button also posts the small blind. The button acts **first** before the flop and **last** after the flop.

[Visual: the seat diagram from Lesson 15, opening on **Heads-up** here, with the 6-player view one tap away. Its numbers show the order of the first turns; a raise sends the action around again.]

**Order of play** always goes to the left (clockwise):
- **Before the flop,** action starts left of the big blind and ends with the blinds: the small blind, then the big blind.
- **After the flop,** the first player left of the button who can still act goes first. **The button acts last.**

**Why acting last is an advantage.** You see what everyone else did before deciding. If they check, you might take the pot with a bet. If someone bets big, you can fold cheaply. Your place in the order is your **position**; acting last is "being in position."

**The big blind's option.** If everyone just calls the big blind before the flop, the big blind can still check (ending the round) or raise. This is the big blind's **option**.

## Lesson 6: All-in

**All-in** means putting in all your chips. You can always call all-in, even with fewer chips than the bet. And whenever you're allowed to raise, you may go all-in even if that's less than a minimum raise.

**When you can't afford a call:** heads-up, your opponent bets 100, and you have only 30. You may still call for 30, all-in. You can only win as much from your opponent as you put in yourself, so **the 70 nobody matched goes back to your opponent**.

[Visual: three steps in a row, replacing the two bars:
1. **Your opponent bets 100.**
2. **You call all-in for 30.**
3. **30 of theirs matches your 30; their other 70 goes back.** The pot gains 30 + 30 = 60 (plus anything already in it).]

**Heads-up, when your opponent is all-in and you owe chips:** you can only call or fold. Nobody is left to match a raise.

**Heads-up, after an all-in is called,** no more betting can happen. The remaining shared cards are dealt out ("run out"), and the showdown decides.

**More than two players?** Two things change, and you'll practice both at a bigger table later:
- The players who still have chips can keep betting against each other. Their matched extra chips go into a **side pot** the all-in player can't win; unmatched chips go back.
- **A short all-in may not reopen the betting.** A player who already bet or raised can raise again only if the amount has gone up by at least a full raise since then. For example: A bets 10, B goes all-in for 16 (only 6 more), and C calls. A may call the 6 or fold, but may not raise. If A had only checked, A could raise. And if several short all-ins add up to a full raise, A may raise again.

## Lesson 7: The showdown

At the showdown, the remaining players show their cards, and the best five-card hand wins.

**Kickers can decide otherwise matching hands.** A **kicker** is a card that doesn't make up the pair or other combination but still counts toward the 5. First compare the pair (or other combination); if it matches, compare the other cards one at a time, highest first.

[Visual: the existing two hands side by side (board A♦ 8♣ 4♠ 9♥ 2♣; you A♠ K♦, your opponent A♣ Q♥), with the deciding cards highlighted: both have a pair of aces, and your K beats their Q. **You win.**]

**Sometimes the kicker is on the board.** Board: A♠ K♣ K♠ Q♦ J♥. You hold A♣ 3♦; your opponent holds A♦ 4♥. Neither the 3 nor the 4 is among the best five cards: both players have A-A-K-K-Q, so you **split the pot**.

**Split pots.** When hands are exactly equal, the pot is shared equally. Any leftover chips go one at a time to the tied players, starting left of the button.

**Showing and mucking.** In a real game, a player who loses at showdown may **muck**: throw their cards away face down without showing them. In this app, all hands at the showdown are shown, so you can learn from them.
